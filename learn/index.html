The Science

Understanding Sea Level Rise

Sea level rise is one of the most measurable and well-documented consequences of climate change. Here's what the science tells us — and what it means for San Diego.

What is it?

What is sea level rise?

Sea level rise refers to the long-term increase in the average height of the ocean's surface. It is measured relative to the land, and it is happening globally — but its effects are felt locally, in places like San Diego's beaches, bays, and waterfront neighborhoods.

The ocean has risen about 8–9 inches (21–24 cm) since 1880, with the rate accelerating in recent decades. Scientists now measure sea level rise using a combination of tide gauges, satellite altimetry, and GPS ground stations.

In San Diego, the tide gauge at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography pier has recorded sea level data since 1924 — one of the longest continuous records on the U.S. West Coast.

Causes

Why is sea level rising?

01

Thermal Expansion

As the ocean absorbs heat from the atmosphere, seawater expands. This thermal expansion accounts for roughly half of observed sea level rise. The ocean has absorbed more than 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases.

02

Melting Ice Sheets

The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass at accelerating rates. Meltwater from these massive reservoirs flows into the ocean, raising sea levels globally. Ice sheet loss is now the fastest-growing contributor to sea level rise.

03

Melting Glaciers

Mountain glaciers worldwide — from the Alps to the Andes to the Himalayas — are retreating rapidly. Their meltwater adds to ocean volume. Glaciers outside the polar ice sheets contribute significantly to near-term sea level rise.

04

Land Subsidence

In some areas, the land itself is sinking due to groundwater extraction, sediment compaction, or tectonic activity. This makes relative sea level rise worse than the global average in affected regions.

San Diego Projections

What scientists project for San Diego

The California Ocean Protection Council and Scripps Institution of Oceanography have developed sea level rise projections specifically for the San Diego region. These are based on global climate models and local tide gauge data.

Low (optimistic)
By 2050~0.5 ft
By 2100~1.0 ft

Assumes aggressive global emissions reductions beginning immediately.

Intermediate
By 2050~1.0 ft
By 2100~2.5 ft

Reflects current global emissions trajectory with moderate mitigation.

High
By 2050~1.5 ft
By 2100~4.0 ft

Assumes continued high emissions and accelerated ice sheet loss.

Extreme (H++)
By 2050~2.0 ft
By 2100~6.6 ft

Low-probability, high-impact scenario used for critical infrastructure planning.

Source: California Ocean Protection Council (2024 update). Projections are for the San Diego region.

Timeline

Key milestones in sea level science

1880

Global sea level baseline established from tide gauge records.

1924

Scripps Institution of Oceanography begins continuous tide gauge measurements in La Jolla.

1993

NASA and CNES launch TOPEX/Poseidon satellite, enabling precise global sea level monitoring from space.

2006

Rate of sea level rise begins accelerating — now rising at about 3.7 mm/year globally.

2018

California adopts updated sea level rise guidance requiring a 6.6 ft planning scenario for critical infrastructure.

2023

Global sea surface temperatures reach record highs; rate of ice sheet loss accelerates.

2050

San Diego projected to experience significant coastal flooding impacts under intermediate scenarios.
