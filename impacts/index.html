Local Impacts

San Diego’s Most Vulnerable Neighborhoods

Sea level rise doesn't affect every community equally. From La Jolla's eroding cliffs to downtown's low-lying waterfront, here's what's at stake — neighborhood by neighborhood.

17 miles of coastline at risk

San Diego County has approximately 70 miles of coastline, with the City of San Diego responsible for roughly 17 miles of beaches, bays, and waterfront. Under intermediate sea level rise projections, significant portions of this coastline face chronic flooding, accelerated erosion, and permanent inundation by 2050.

The impacts are not uniform. Low-lying areas like Mission Bay and the downtown Embarcadero face flooding risk. Coastal bluff communities like La Jolla and Ocean Beach face accelerated erosion. And communities along the Silver Strand face threats from both sides — ocean and bay.

La Jolla & Torrey Pines

La Jolla's iconic sandstone bluffs are eroding at an accelerating rate. The cliffs that define the neighborhood's character are retreating, threatening homes, roads, and the beloved Cove.

The sandstone cliffs of La Jolla are naturally soft and susceptible to wave action. Sea level rise increases the frequency and intensity of wave impacts at the cliff base. Torrey Pines State Beach could disappear under high sea level rise scenarios without major intervention.

Mission Bay

Mission Bay Park — San Diego's 4,600-acre aquatic playground — sits at near sea level. Rising bay waters threaten the park's beaches, facilities, and the surrounding residential neighborhoods.

Mission Bay is a low-lying enclosed bay with minimal elevation above current sea level. Even modest sea level rise will cause chronic flooding of park beaches and facilities. The city has begun planning for adaptation, including potential managed retreat and elevation of critical infrastructure.

Downtown & Embarcadero

Downtown San Diego's waterfront sits just feet above sea level. The Embarcadero, convention center, and Seaport Village face increasing flood risk from both sea level rise and intensified storm surges.

Much of downtown's waterfront was built on filled land, leaving it at low elevation. The Port of San Diego has begun incorporating sea level rise into long-term planning, including raised promenades and flood-resilient landscaping.

Ocean Beach & Point Loma

Ocean Beach faces the dual threat of beach erosion and bluff retreat. The beloved OB pier and beachfront community are at increasing risk as sand supplies diminish and sea levels rise.

Ocean Beach has experienced significant sand loss over recent decades. Higher water levels push wave energy further up the beach profile, while Point Loma's lower-elevation areas face flooding that will become more frequent and severe.

Coronado & Silver Strand

The Silver Strand — the narrow barrier spit connecting Coronado to Imperial Beach — faces flooding from both the ocean and San Diego Bay. It is among the most vulnerable landforms in the region.

With ocean on one side and San Diego Bay on the other, the strand faces flooding from both directions during major storms. Highway 75 is a critical transportation corridor whose disruption could isolate Coronado from the mainland.

The response

What is San Diego doing about it?

The City of San Diego, the Port, the County, and individual coastal communities are beginning adaptation planning — but the scale of investment needed far exceeds current budgets.

Take action

Educating San Diegans about our changing coast. Understanding sea level rise is the first step toward protecting the places we love.
